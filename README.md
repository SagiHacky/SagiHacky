I'm queer, I'm a contradiction, and I got somewhat good at code along the way, though that part is honestly the least interesting thing about me. What matters more to me is individual freedom, and I don't mean it as some abstract word you put on a badge — I mean it as something you have to keep re-deciding to do, every day, in how you let people around you just exist without you needing to manage them.

I could try to make being tender about autonomy and being furious about injustice sound like they resolve into one clean idea, but they don't, not really, and I've stopped trying to force that resolution. Both are just true of me at once.

I write code more or less the way I write everything else, which is with a question underneath it I can't put down — who does this actually serve, and who does it quietly cost. I used to think neutrality in tech was some kind of virtue. I don't think that anymore. At this point I think it's mostly cowardice that got itself a better PR team.

My reference points are existentialism, feminist theory, and anarcho-capitalism, and yes, I know that combination sounds like it shouldn't hold together — I get asked about it a lot, and I still don't have a short answer for it, only a long one, so here's the short version anyway: voluntary exchange, decentralized everything, consent treated as infrastructure and not as an afterthought you bolt on later. State coercion, as I currently see it, is a bug and not a feature. I reserve the right to have thought about this more by the time you're reading it.

## `// what I actually do`

Right now I'm building a cybersecurity project, and if I had to state the thesis of it — because I do tend to think in theses, it's a habit from writing too many essays — it's that "security" shouldn't just mean locking things down. There's a whole culture in this field that treats the user as a threat to be managed instead of a person to be protected, and I genuinely can't stand it. I want to build things that are private by default, honest about what they can't do, made for people who have actual, specific reasons to need protection, not hypothetical ones somebody invented for a pitch deck.

Hacktivism, the way I understand and actually practice it, was never about chaos for its own sake. It's about accountability — refusing to let power do its business somewhere nobody is looking.

When I'm not writing code I'm usually reading, and honestly these three show up in how I think about security more than any actual security training ever did: Camus, because life having no built-in meaning is, to me, exactly where freedom starts rather than where it ends. Dune, because it's basically a long case study in how religion and politics manufacture consent without anyone noticing it happening. Tolkien, because you can build an entire world if you care enough about the small, tedious details of it — which, if you think about it, is also just good threat modeling.

## `// things I actually think`

Surveillance gets marketed to us as a security feature. It's a control feature. I think most of the industry knows the difference and has just decided not to say it out loud.

Feminism has reshaped how I think about security more than anything with the word "security" in its title ever managed to. If your threat model doesn't put the most vulnerable person in the room at the center of it, it isn't a threat model at all — it's a liability document wearing a threat model's clothes.

Patriarchy hurts everyone stuck inside it, including men. The ones who let themselves be vulnerable instead of performing toughness aren't doing the weaker thing, whatever anyone tells them. I'd argue it's the harder road, not the easier one.

Decentralization is worth defending even when — and let's be honest, this is most of the time — it's slower and more annoying than just letting one server handle everything.

Open source is a start. It is nowhere close to an ending.

I don't think neutral tools exist, and here's the actual reasoning behind that, not just the bare claim: someone always made a design decision, usually several of them, and by the time the tool reaches you those decisions have gone invisible. That invisibility is the political part. That's the part nobody wants to talk about.

## `// the rest of it`

I'm a sensitive person, biologically, mostly, and I don't have a ton of control over that, which took me a while to actually be okay with instead of fighting it. It means a sunset, or one specific injustice, or a sentence that landed slightly wrong can wreck an entire afternoon for me, easily. It also means I get overwhelmed and need more time to process than a lot of people around me seem to need, and yes, I'm aware how that sounds written down. It's also, I'm fairly sure, exactly why I'm actually good at listening to people when it counts. Empathy is a gift and it's a cost, in roughly equal parts, and most days I genuinely still can't tell you which part is winning.

I'm an introvert, and I don't say that to sound self-deprecating about it — I guard my quiet like it's something worth guarding, because it is, full stop. That doesn't mean I dislike people. It means I need to be alone first, before I can be around anyone properly, or I'm not actually there with them, not really.

I write short stories, most of them unfinished, some that nobody but me will ever read, and I've made peace with that — writing is mostly just how I untangle a thought that would otherwise sit knotted somewhere I can't reach it any other way. At some point I want to make a film, maybe something small, maybe not, I don't know yet — I want to tell a story through image and rhythm instead of only through words, and I honestly can't tell if that's real ambition or just a nice idea I like having around.

I photograph the things most people walk straight past without noticing — a crack running through a wall, light sitting flat on water, someone's face in the half-second before they realize they're being watched. It's a way of telling a story without needing language for it at all. When I look back at my own old photos I'm not really looking at what was in the frame — I'm looking at who I was that day, what I cared about back then, how the world looked to me before it looked like this. They work like a mirror I never meant to build.

Music runs under basically everything I do, and it splits pretty cleanly for me: older songs, the ones carrying weight and built-in nostalgia; ambient noise for when I need to just breathe and be nowhere specific for a while; the Heartstopper soundtrack for one particular feeling I don't have a better word for than nervous and hopeful at the same time. Good music, to me, is whatever ends up being exactly what the moment needed, before I even knew that's what I needed.

Nature is an escape, full stop, no complications — trees, mountains, a river, or honestly even just an empty field, and I feel free there in a way I don't manage most other places. It doesn't ask anything of me. No performance required. I'll sit on a hill and stare at nothing in particular for way longer than is productive. I'll walk for hours without a destination in mind. It's humbling in the actual sense of the word — being reminded how small I am doesn't feel bad most of the time, it feels closer to being told I'm part of something big enough that my usual worries just don't scale up to it.

I think about the universe more than is probably healthy, if I'm honest — billions of galaxies, each one with billions of stars, and here we are, on this one small rock, somewhere in the middle of an amount of space I can't actually picture even when I try. Some days that's terrifying. Other days it's the most freeing thought I have access to, because on that scale basically every stressor and every bit of social pressure I'm carrying is close to nothing at all. We get one short stretch of time inside something close to infinite. Which is, I think, exactly the reason that stretch should be made to mean something.
